Methodological Breakdown

The Fundamental Problem: Why Classical Statistics Fails in Space

The Classical Illusion: Traditional statistics (OLS regression, ANOVA, standard machine learning) assumes every observation is an independent coin flip (i.i.d.). In real geography, this assumption completely collapses!

The Geographic Reality

  • Shared Realities: Adjacent communities share the same weather, infrastructure, economic shocks, and social networks.
  • Spatial Clustering: Wealth, disease, and poverty naturally cluster into geographic patches rather than scattering randomly across space.

The Statistical Danger

  • Fake Degrees of Freedom: 1,000 geographic units do NOT contain 1,000 independent pieces of information.
  • False Discoveries: Standard software underestimates error margins, producing artificially tiny p-values that declare random noise to be "statistically significant"!
Core Axiom

Tobler's First Law: Spatial Dependence & Distance Decay

"Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things."

, Waldo R. Tobler (1970)

Direct Intuition

  • Distance Decay: Influence fades smoothly as geographic distance increases.
  • Information Redundancy: Sampling two houses on the same street gives you almost identical socioeconomic information.
  • Endogenous Spillover: What happens in your neighbor's backyard directly changes the value and conditions of your own.

The Rule of Space:

You cannot simply throw geographic data into standard statistical models without explicitly modeling the distance decay that connects them!

Aggregation Sensitivity

The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP)

Statistical findings on geographic data depend directly on how boundaries are drawn, rather than the true underlying facts:

1. The Scale Effect

Aggregating data from small units to large units averages out local variation:

  • As zones get larger, data variance shrinks and correlations artificially inflate!
  • A relationship with a weak correlation (R^2 = 0.15) at neighborhood level can magically look like an ironclad trend (R^2 = 0.75) at state level.

2. The Zone Effect

Holding unit size constant, just moving the boundary lines yields completely different results:

  • This is the mathematical foundation of political gerrymandering.
  • Key Takeaway: Always verify that findings hold across multiple geographic scales!
Analytical Traps

Spatial Fallacies: Ecological & Aspatial Fallacies

The Ecological Fallacy

The Mistake: Assuming that an aggregate trend observed at the district or state level applies to the individuals living inside it.

Famous Example: Robinson (1950) proved that states with high immigrant populations had higher literacy rates, but individual immigrants had lower literacy rates. Immigrants simply settled in wealthier, more literate states!

The Aspatial Fallacy

The Mistake: Analyzing geographic data while stripping away all coordinates, geography, and spatial context.

Why It Fails: If you randomly shuffle all locations on a map, standard OLS regression produces the exact same numbers! It is 100% blind to spatial segregation and geographic clustering.

Visual Diagnostic

Spatial vs. Aspatial Distributions: The Blind Spot

Two datasets can have identical summary statistics and histograms, yet represent completely opposite geographic realities:

Aspatial Histogram: Only sees numbers in a bucket (f(y)), blind to where high and low values sit.
Spatial Topology: Instantly reveals segregation, geographic pockets, and sharp regional divides.
Process Regimes

Stationarity vs. Spatial Heterogeneity (Non-Stationarity)

Spatial Stationarity (Global Process)

Assumes the rules of the world are identical everywhere:

  • A single global average slope (beta) governs the entire map.
  • Assumes an extra year of schooling produces the exact same income bump in an isolated rural village as in a central financial district.

Spatial Heterogeneity (Local Process)

Recognizes that relationships change across geography:

The Reality: Economic, cultural, and geographic differences cause relationships to drift across space. One global slope averages positive and negative regional effects into meaninglessness!

Spatial Mathematics

The Spatial Weights Matrix (W): Formalizing Neighborhoods

1. Mathematical Role

W is an n × n matrix quantizing spatial relationships between all n geographical units.

Diagonal is zero (w_ii = 0): no unit is its own neighbour.

2. Strict Exogeneity

W must be constructed strictly from spatial geometry or geographic distance.

Never use economic or endogenous outcome variables to define W.

3. Matrix Sparsity

Most spatial units only contact a few neighbours; W contains mostly zeroes.

Efficient sparse matrix storage enables fast computational estimation.

Geometric Criteria

Spatial Weight Topologies: Contiguity, Distance & k-NN

Contiguity (Borders)

Based on shared boundaries:

  • Rook: Units share a common edge (like a chess rook).
  • Queen: Units share an edge OR a single corner point (like a chess queen).

Distance Decay

Continuous decay over space:

  • Nearby points have maximum influence; influence decays smoothly toward zero as distance grows.
  • Ideal for point facilities and environmental plumes.

k-Nearest Neighbors

Fixed neighbor counts:

  • Every unit gets exactly k neighbors (e.g. k=8).
  • Prevents isolated rural areas from being cut off, and prevents dense cities from being overwhelmed.
Matrix Algebra

Row Standardization: Creating Neighborhood Averages

The Intuition: Raw neighbor counts vary (one county has 2 neighbors, another has 10). Row-standardization divides each neighbor weight by the row sum so all weights in each row add up to exactly 1.0!

Why Row-Standardization is Essential

  • Equal Footing: Units with 2 neighbors and units with 12 neighbors are evaluated on the exact same relative scale.
  • Neighborhood Averaging: Multiplying a variable by row-standardized W yields the exact weighted average of its neighbors!

Mathematical Invertibility Range

  • Row-standardization forces the maximum eigenvalue to be 1.0.
  • This guarantees that the spatial feedback parameter rho is stable and invertible between -1.0 lt rho lt 1.0.
Core Mechanism

The Spatial Lag Operator: Computing [Wy]_i

The Big Idea: The Spatial Lag [Wy]_i is simply the Neighborhood Average. It answers: "What is the average condition of the communities surrounding Unit i?"

How It Operates in Models

  • Peer Pressure & Contagion: If everyone around you adopts solar panels, you are much more likely to adopt them too.
  • Exogenous Shocks: If neighboring farms suffer a drought, your local market prices change immediately.

Simple Calculation Example

If Unit 1 has two neighbors with wealth values of 80 and 100, its row-standardized weights are 0.5 and 0.5:

Unit 1's spatial lag is 90!

Global Autocorrelation

Global Spatial Autocorrelation: Moran's I

The Spatial Correlation Coefficient: Moran's I measures the overall degree of spatial clustering across the entire map, exactly like Pearson's r measures linear correlation.

Positive Clustering (I > 0)

Clustering: High values cluster near high values; low values cluster near low values (segregation, regional wealth).

Random Pattern (I ≈ 0)

Randomness: Values are scattered like white noise; no geographic pattern exists.

Dispersion / Checkerboard (I < 0)

Dispersion: Checkerboard pattern; high values surrounded by low values (rare in nature).

Local Decompositions

Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA)

Beyond the Global Average: Global Moran tells you if clustering exists. Local Moran (Iᵢ) pinpoints where the clusters and anomalies are on the map!

What LISA Detects

  • Cluster Mapping: Distinguishes statistically significant Hotspots (HH) from Coldspots (LL).
  • Outlier Detection: Flags anomalous enclaves that defy their regional surroundings.

Controlling False Discoveries

Multiple Comparisons Trap

When running tests on thousands of units simultaneously, pure chance generates false clusters. Apply False Discovery Rate (FDR) corrections to guarantee statistical validity!

Hotspot Statistics

Getis-Ord Hotspot Analysis (Gi*)

Direct Hotspot & Coldspot z-Scores: The Getis-Ord Gi* statistic calculates a standard normal score for every location, directly telling you if it is part of a statistically significant cluster.

Direct Decision Rules

  • z gt +1.96 (p < 0.05): Statistically significant Hotspot.
  • z lt -1.96 (p < 0.05): Statistically significant Coldspot.
  • Between -1.96 and +1.96: Random geographic noise.

Difference from Local Moran

Getis-Ord includes the unit itself in its neighborhood (w_ii gt 0), making it ideal for detecting hot and cold cores, while Local Moran excels at finding spatial outliers.

Regression Baseline

Classical Linear Regression: The Aspatial Baseline

The Four Core Elements

  • y: Outcome variable we seek to understand.
  • X: Explanatory predictor variables.
  • beta: Global slope (assumed identical everywhere).
  • Error term: Random, independent noise (residuals).

The Foundational Goal

Find the best-fit line that minimizes the sum of squared prediction errors. Valid ONLY when residuals are completely independent!

Estimator Mechanics

How OLS Fits the Line: Intuition & Assumptions

The OLS Solution

OLS finds the unique mathematical angle that projects y onto X with the minimum possible squared residual distance.

  • Requires predictors to be independent of the error term.
  • Assumes one single global rule explains the entire territory.

The Blind Spot

OLS has no concept of geography. If an omitted factor (like rainfall or local culture) clusters in space, OLS will silently absorb it into the error term, violating its own core assumptions!

Statistical Inference

Hypothesis Testing in OLS: The Isolation Assumption

Confidence & Significance

  • Standard Error: The margin of uncertainty around our slope estimates.
  • t-statistic & p-value: Tests whether a predictor has a real effect (p < 0.05).

The Aspatial Isolation Trap

OLS strictly assumes that changing a variable in County A affects only County A. The cross-border spillover to County B is assumed to be exactly zero!

Theoretical Violation

The Gauss-Markov Breakdown in Geographic Space

Gauss-Markov Theorem: OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) ONLY IF errors are independent and homoscedastic (spherical disturbances).

Why It Breaks in Space

  • Omitted regional variables (climate, soil, local policies) cause adjacent errors to be correlated: neighbouring error terms become positively correlated.
  • The disturbance covariance matrix becomes non-spherical!

The Result: OLS is No Longer BLUE

OLS ceases to be optimal. Standard errors become invalid, confidence intervals become fiction, and hypothesis tests produce massive false discoveries.

Inferential Failure

What Happens When You Ignore Spatial Correlation in OLS?

Case A: Spatial Error Dependence

  • Coefficients hatbeta are unbiased, but inefficient.
  • Reported standard errors are severely deflated.
  • Software declares variables "highly significant" when they are merely reflecting shared regional noise!

Case B: Spatial Lag Dependence

  • Omitting peer feedback (Wy) causes severe omitted variable bias.
  • Coefficients hatbeta are both biased and inconsistent.
  • Gathering more data does NOT fix the bias!

Model Taxonomy

Spatial Models: Choosing the Right Tool

When OLS fails due to spatial correlation, we switch to dedicated spatial statistics models:

Spatial Lag (SAR)

Peer Feedback: Direct behavioral contagion, diffusion, market competition.

Outcome = Spatial Lag of Outcome + Predictors + Error

Spatial Error (SEM)

Regional Noise: Unobserved climate shocks, soil quality, shared power grids.

Outcome = Predictors + Spatially Correlated Error

Spatial Durbin (SDM)

Dual Spillovers: Both peer outcomes (Wy) AND neighbor characteristics (WX).

Outcome = Spatial Lag + Predictors + Predictor Spillovers + Error

Autoregressive Lag

Spatial Lag Model (SAR): Modeling Contagion & Peer Effects

When to use SAR: The outcome in each location is directly caused by outcomes in neighbouring locations, contagion, diffusion, peer pressure, or market competition.

Real-World Examples

  • Retail Geomarketing: A new superstore raises revenue at nearby competitor branches too (foot traffic spills). With spatial autoregressive coefficient rhoho = 0.45, multiplier = frac11-0.45 = 1.82, every ₦1M campaign generates ₦1.82M total network revenue.
  • Crime Displacement: Policing crackdown in Zone A pushes crime into Zones B & C. SAR captures this spatial contagion directly.
  • Epidemic Diffusion: Malaria incidence in one district drives incidence in adjacent districts through shared markets and migration routes.

Interpretation Guide

Spatial Lag Coefficient (rho = 0.45) means:

45% of the outcome in any location is driven by the weighted average outcome of its neighbours, before any predictor effects. The total system multiplier amplifies every local intervention by frac11-rho.

SAR vs. OLS: What changes?

OLS reports only the direct local effect. SAR adds the indirect ripple chain through neighbours. Ignoring SAR when it is present biases all hatbeta estimates because Wy becomes an omitted variable.

Error Autoregressive

Spatial Error Model (SEM): Correcting for Invisible Regional Shocks

When to use SEM: The outcome is not contagious between neighbours, but unmeasured regional forces (climate, soil, governance) simultaneously affect clusters of adjacent units, making their errors correlated.

Real-World Examples

  • Agricultural Yield: A model of crop yields includes rainfall, temperature, and fertiliser, but omits soil quality, which clusters geographically. Residuals are correlated across adjacent farms because all share the same soil gradient. SEM filters this out.
  • Public Health: An OLS of maternal mortality reports p < 0.01 for clinic distance. But Moran's I on residuals = 0.42, clustered governance quality is the real driver. SEM corrects the inflated significance and gives honest p-values.

Interpretation Guide & SAR vs. SEM

Ask: Is the outcome contagious?

Yes (disease spreads person to person, crime displaces) → SAR.
No (nearby districts both suffer the same hidden drought, not from each other) → SEM.

What Error Autocorrelation (lambda) tells you:

lambda = 0.6: 60% of unexplained variation in each unit can be predicted from its neighbours' unexplained variation. OLS standard errors were severely underestimated, all significance tests were inflated.

Nested Spillovers

Spatial Durbin Model (SDM): Capturing Both Outcome & Context Spillovers

When to use SDM: Not only does the outcome spread to neighbours (like SAR), but your neighbours' own characteristics, their investment, wealth, education, also directly affect your outcome. Use SDM when both channels operate.

Real-World Example, Hospital Investment Spillovers

SDM of child mortality with hospital investment as predictor gives:
Direct effect = 0.42, building a hospital in District A reduces mortality there by 4.2pp per 10% investment.
Indirect (spillover) = 0.31, adjacent districts also benefit as residents cross boundaries for care.
Total effect = 0.73, raw coefficient from OLS was 0.39; SDM shows you missed 47% of the true ROI!

Why SDM is the Safest Default

LeSage & Pace (2009) proved:

SDM remains unbiased even when relevant spatially-correlated variables are omitted, because the WXgamma term absorbs their influence. When uncertain between SAR and SEM, SDM encompasses both.

Other applied uses:

School funding spillovers (neighbouring districts share teachers & students). Infrastructure investment (roads in adjacent LGAs raise your market access). Technology diffusion where both adoption rates and contextual factors matter.

Impact Decomposition

Reading Spatial Models: Why hatbeta Is NOT the Marginal Effect

The critical mistake: In SAR and SDM, reading hatbeta as the marginal effect is wrong. Because outcomes ripple through the network, the true impact has three components, and the raw coefficient captures none of them correctly.

Direct Effect

Impact of changing x_ik on y_i, including the feedback loop that travels i to j to i through neighbours. Slightly larger than hatbeta due to echo effects.

Example: Building a library in District A raises literacy in District A by 4.2pp (not 3.9 from raw hatbeta).

Indirect Effect (Spillover)

Cross-border impact on all other districts j neq i from a change in District A. This is the true spatial externality, the benefit that crosses administrative lines.

Example: The same library raises literacy in surrounding districts by 3.1pp as students and teachers travel across boundaries.

Total Effect

Direct + Indirect. The full societal return on the investment. Policymakers who only read hatbeta underestimate the true ROI by the entire indirect component.

Example: Total literacy gain = 4.2 + 3.1 = 7.3pp per 10% investment, 87% larger than hatbeta suggested!

Local Variation

Spatial Heterogeneity: When the Same Policy Works Differently Across Space

The global average trap: OLS, SAR, and SEM all estimate one slope hatbeta for the entire study area. If the true effect varies strongly across geography, this global average describes every place equally poorly, and no place accurately.

Concrete Examples of Drift

  • Clinic Distance & Mortality: In rural northeast Nigeria, distance to clinic is a life-or-death predictor (hatbeta approx 0.74). In dense urban Lagos, it is near zero, because substitutes abound. A global hatbeta = 0.38 describes no specific place correctly.
  • Fertiliser Subsidies: Strong positive yield response in the riverine south where water supports uptake; near-zero or negative in the hyper-arid north where soil chemistry limits absorption.
  • Hidden Cancellation: If an effect is +0.8 in the North and -0.6 in the South, global OLS reports hatbeta approx +0.1, a near-zero average masking two strong, opposite policy regimes!

Detecting Heterogeneity

AICc Model Comparison Test

Fit GWR and compute its AICc. If AICc of GWR is at least 3 points lower than OLS AICc, local variation is statistically confirmed, the global model is masking important geographic heterogeneity and local models are needed.

Local Regression

Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR): One Model Per Location

The big idea: GWR fits a separate, locally calibrated regression at every geographic coordinate. Each community gets its own slope estimate, not the global average, but the slope that best fits that community and its immediate neighbours.

Applied Example, Child Mortality, Nigeria

A GWR model of child mortality on clinic access shows:
Rural NE: Distance coefficient = 0.74, strong, significant (t-statistic = 4.2)
Urban Lagos: Distance coefficient = 0.09, non-significant (t-statistic = 1.1)
Global OLS: hatbeta = 0.38, averages positive and near-zero into a meaningless national figure.

The GWR map tells planners exactly where to build new facilities for maximum impact.

How to Interpret GWR Output

  • Parameter map: Each district coloured by its local local coefficient, a continuous surface showing strength and direction of the relationship across geography.
  • Significance masking: Only show coefficients where |t| > 1.96 (p < 0.05). Greyed-out areas mean the relationship is not statistically detectable there.
  • Residual Moran's I: After GWR, residuals should show I approx 0, confirming the local model absorbed the spatial structure the global model missed.
Kernel & Bandwidth

GWR Kernels & Bandwidth: How Local Is "Local"?

The bandwidth controls how many nearby observations influence each local regression, too narrow and you overfit noise, too wide and you recreate a global model.

Kernel Types

Adaptive Bisquare (Standard)

Adapts radius to local data density. In Lagos (dense), the bandwidth shrinks to capture fine-grained urban variation. In Bornu (sparse), it expands to borrow information from farther away. Use for most geographic analyses.

Fixed Gaussian

Same physical radius everywhere. Use when the process itself is fixed-scale (e.g. disease transmission radius = 50km). Risk: leaves sparse rural areas data-starved.

Bandwidth = Geographic Scale of the Process

Optimal Bandwidth via AICc

Software automatically searches over bandwidth values and selects the one minimising the Corrected AIC. AICc penalises both over-fitting (tiny bandwidth) and over-smoothing (giant bandwidth).

Interpretation of final bw*:

If optimal bw* = 40 in a 774-LGA study, each local regression uses its 40 nearest neighbours as primary data, a hyper-local scale. If bw* = 600, the process is essentially global.

Multiscale Modeling

Multiscale GWR (MGWR): Each Predictor at Its Own Scale

The limitation of GWR: GWR forces all variables to share a single bandwidth. But clinic access varies at the village level while national governance quality varies at the country level, forcing the same scale on both corrupts both estimates.

Applied Example, Child Stunting Analysis

An MGWR of child stunting across 30 countries reveals individual bandwidth estimates:
Open defecation rate: bandwidth = 31 (hyper-local, village sanitation norms change sharply between adjacent communities)
Health facility density: bandwidth = 124 (regional, facility catchments span multiple districts)
Climate variability: bandwidth = 820 (near-global, rainfall patterns are smooth across large zones)

Standard GWR at bandwidth = 200 would have over-smoothed defecation and under-smoothed climate, biasing all three estimates.

Reading MGWR Output

Bandwidth as a scientific finding

Small bwₖ: Predictor k operates at a hyper-local scale, it varies sharply across short distances. Target interventions at community level.
Large bandwidth approaching total sample size n: Predictor k is essentially global, its coefficient barely changes across the map. Can be represented as a standard OLS term.

GWR vs MGWR comparison:

Always compare AICc. If AICc of MGWR is at least 3 points lower than GWR AICc, the multi-scale structure is statistically meaningful and MGWR should be reported.

Map Interpretation

Reading GWR & MGWR Parameter Maps: A Complete Guide

What Each Map Shows

  • Colour = coefficient strength & direction: A blue-to-red diverging palette where blue = strong negative relationship, red = strong positive. A district coloured dark red for hatbeta_textpoverty means poverty is a very powerful driver of the outcome there.
  • Grey / hatched areas = not significant: |t| < 1.96. The relationship exists but cannot be distinguished from noise at that location. Never interpret or act on these areas.
  • Sharp boundaries = process discontinuities: Rapid colour transitions across adjacent districts signal a governance boundary, ecological threshold, or ethnic/cultural border where the relationship regime changes.

Applied Interpretation Example

GWR of malaria on rainfall: the northeast shows Rainfall coefficient = 0.82 (significant), every 10mm extra rainfall raises incidence 8.2 cases/1000. The southeast shows hatbeta_textrain = -0.14 (significant), higher rainfall dilutes mosquito density via flooding. The middle belt shows grey (not significant), rainfall has no detectable marginal effect there, likely dominated by stagnant water bodies regardless of rain.

Residual sanity check

After GWR, compute Moran's I on the residuals. If still significant, GWR has not fully absorbed the spatial structure, consider adding predictors or switching to MGWR.

Comparative Paradigm

Spatial Statistics & GWR vs. Machine Learning: Which Should You Choose?

CriteriaSpatial Statistics / GWRMachine Learning (RF / XGBoost)
Primary Goal Explanation & Policy: Explaining why and estimating elasticity (partial y / partial x). Pure Prediction: Maximizing accuracy on haty.
Space Representation Continuous distance-decay kernels w(d) based on Tobler's law. Rectangular coordinate splits (X, Y) creating artificial step cliffs.
Interpretability Local slopes local coefficient(u,v) with standard errors and p-values. Black-box feature importance without signs or significance.
Spatial Leakage Explicitly modeled via spatial lags (Wy) and spatial block CV. High risk: standard k-fold CV leaks spatial proximity.

Choose GWR & Spatial Statistics when:

  • You need to explain cause and effect (e.g. "By how much does a 1km reduction in clinic distance improve health?").
  • Policy, legal, or academic peer review requires confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

Choose Machine Learning when:

  • The sole objective is pure prediction at unobserved locations (e.g. satellite image interpolation).
  • Complex high-order non-linear feature interactions dominate.
Methodological Workflow

Spatial Statistics Model Selection Protocol & Diagnostics

1

1. OLS Baseline

Fit standard OLS. Test residual Moran's I. If p ≥ 0.05, retain OLS.

2

2. LM Tests

Run LM-Lag & LM-Error to check spatial lag vs error dependence.

3

3. Model Choice

Select SAR if LM-Lag is significant, SEM if LM-Error, or SDM if both.

4

4. Test GWR

Run GWR/MGWR. If AICc drops by > 3 vs OLS, local drift is confirmed.

Calculate Direct, Indirect, and Total effects for policy decisions.
