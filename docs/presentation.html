Foundations

Why Spatial Statistics? The Breakdown of Classical Theory

In standard data science and econometrics, observations are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.):

$$\text{Cov}(y_i, y_j) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \neq j, \quad \epsilon \sim \text{i.i.d. } N(0, \sigma^2)$$

The Geographic Reality

When analyzing geographic units (e.g. 9,308 administrative wards), this assumption collapses due to Tobler's First Law of Geography (1970):
"Everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things."

Consequences of Aspatial Models

• Biased Estimates: Attribute neighbor spillovers falsely to local features.
• Artificially Low Standard Errors: Inflated $t$-statistics producing widespread Type-I false discoveries.
• Stationarity Trap: Forcing a single global parameter across heterogeneous regions.

Pitfall #1

Spatial Fallacy 1: The Fallacy of the State Average

Policymakers routinely allocate capital based on national or state-level summary aggregates:

$$\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n y_i \implies \text{Completely masks spatial variance within the boundary}$$

The Numerical Distortion

A state may record an affluent mean Relative Wealth Index of +0.42, yet contain 15 rural wards suffering acute structural poverty (-0.85) alongside a single hyper-wealthy urban enclave (+1.65). The aggregate average renders vulnerable populations statistically invisible.

Real-Life Applied Case

Universal Healthcare Budgeting: Directing state grants based on statewide maternal mortality averages starves peripheral rural wards while over-subsidizing tertiary hospitals in state capitals.

Pitfall #2

Spatial Fallacy 2: The Spatial Spillover Blindspot

Aspatial program evaluation assumes interventions in Ward $A$ remain isolated inside Ward $A$:

$$\Delta y_A = \beta \Delta X_A \quad \text{(Ignores inter-ward geographic feedback loops)}$$

The Two Spatial Phenomena

  • Spatial Contagion (Epidemics / Crime): Disease transmission in one ward directly causes infection surges in contiguous neighbor wards.
  • Economic Agglomeration (Trade / Retail): A wholesale market built in Ward $A$ creates customer traffic, logistics suppliers, and demand in Wards $B$ and $C$.

Real-Life Applied Case

Wholesale Market Construction: An aspatial ROI model calculates only municipal tax generated within Ward $A$, ignoring 141% additional secondary wealth generated across adjacent boundary wards!

Pitfall #3

Spatial Fallacy 3: The Boundary Trap & MAUP

The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) dictates that statistical results change dramatically when boundaries are shifted or aggregated:

1. The Scale Effect

Aggregating 9,308 wards up to 774 Local Government Areas (LGAs) artificially inflates bivariate correlation coefficients ($r$) from 0.31 to 0.74. High aggregation smooths out local variance and creates spurious associations.

2. The Zoning Effect

Redrawing administrative district shapes while holding unit count constant alters statistical sign and magnitude (e.g. electoral gerrymandering or redrawn health catchment areas changing disease rate calculations).

Methodological Antidote: Always model at the finest operational administrative unit (Ward level) and verify scale stability using multi-level or geographically weighted techniques.

Methodology

Traditional vs. Spatial Exploratory Data Analysis

Spatial EDA integrates geographic topology, proximity matrices, and spatial lag operators into exploratory discovery:

Spatial EDA

Spatial Outliers & Local Geographic Anomalies

In standard EDA, an outlier is defined solely by distance from the global distribution mean ($z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$):

Aspatial Outlier (Global View)

A ward is flagged as an outlier only if its wealth or disease rate exceeds $3\sigma$ from the entire national average. Misses localized deprivation inside wealthy metropolitan regions.

Spatial Outlier (Local View)

A unit that differs radically from its immediate spatial neighbors, regardless of where it falls in the national histogram:

  • High-Low (Island): An affluent commercial corridor surrounded by structural poverty.
  • Low-High (Sink): An excluded slum community trapped inside a wealthy city core.
Spatial Weights

Spatial Weights Matrix ($W$): Contiguity vs. Nearest Neighbours

The spatial weights matrix $W$ encodes geographic connectivity among all $n$ observational units:

$$W = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & w_{12} & \dots & w_{1n} \\ w_{21} & 0 & \dots & w_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ w_{n1} & w_{n2} & \dots & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad w_{ij}^* = \frac{w_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^n w_{ik}} \implies \sum_{j=1}^n w_{ij}^* = 1$$

Contiguity vs. $K$-Nearest Neighbours

  • Queen Contiguity: Units sharing an edge or point vertex are neighbors ($w_{ij}=1$). The Island Problem: Islands or exclaves have zero neighbors, breaking row standardization.
  • $K$-Nearest Neighbours ($k$-NN): Every ward is connected to its $k$ closest centroids. Guarantees uniform degrees and eliminates island isolates.
  • Inverse Distance: $w_{ij} = d_{ij}^{-\alpha}$ with distance band threshold.

Real-Life Applied Use Case

Modeling Inter-Ward Trade Networks: Because administrative wards vary vastly in polygon area (from $0.2 \text{ km}^2$ in urban centers to $2,500 \text{ km}^2$ in rural areas), using $k\text{-NN} (k=5)$ provides robust, scale-invariant spatial weights.

Spatial Lag

The Spatial Lag Operator ($Wy$): Local Neighborhood Averages

Multiplying row-standardized $W$ by variable vector $y$ computes the spatial lag $[Wy]_i$:

$$[Wy]_i = \sum_{j=1}^n w_{ij}^* y_j = w_{i1}^* y_1 + w_{i2}^* y_2 + \dots + w_{in}^* y_n$$

Step-by-Step Numerical Example

Suppose Ward $i$ has 4 neighbors with wealth index scores: [+0.20, +0.60, -0.10, +0.50].

Under row standardization, each neighbor weight is $w_{ij}^* = 0.25$:

$$[Wy]_i = 0.25(0.20) + 0.25(0.60) + 0.25(-0.10) + 0.25(0.50) = \mathbf{+0.30}$$

The spatial lag $[Wy]_i$ represents the average environmental context surrounding unit $i$.

Moran Scatterplot Representation

Plotting standardized focal values $z$ on the horizontal axis against neighborhood lag $Wz$ on the vertical axis yields the Moran Scatterplot. The regression slope of this scatterplot is exactly equal to Global Moran's $I$!

Global Autocorrelation

Global Spatial Autocorrelation: Moran's $I$ Statistic

Quantifies whether a variable clusters spatially across the entire study region:

$$I = \frac{n}{\sum_{i} \sum_{j} w_{ij}} \cdot \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n w_{ij}(y_i - \bar{y})(y_j - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2}, \quad E[I] = -\frac{1}{n-1} \approx 0$$

Statistical Significance & Permutation Test

  • Compute reference distribution by randomly shuffling spatial coordinates $M=999$ times.
  • Calculate pseudo $p$-value: $p = \frac{M_{\text{extreme}} + 1}{M + 1}$.
  • Empirical Result: Relative Wealth Index yields $I = 0.614$ ($p = 0.001$), proving extreme spatial clustering!

Value Range & Interpretation

  • $I \gt 0$ (Positive Autocorrelation): Similar values cluster together (hotspots and coldspots).
  • $I \approx 0$ (Spatial Randomness): Spatial distribution is random noise.
  • $I \lt 0$ (Negative Autocorrelation): Dissimilar values neighbor each other (checkerboard pattern).
Local Clusters

Anselin Local Moran (LISA): Decomposing Global Clustering

Global Moran's $I$ confirms clustering exists; Anselin Local Moran ($I_i$) maps where each cluster is located:

$$I_i = \frac{y_i - \bar{y}}{s^2} \sum_{j=1}^n w_{ij} (y_j - \bar{y})$$

The Four Core Quadrants ($p \lt 0.05$)

  • High-High (Hotspot): High values surrounded by high values. Priority for commercial flagship investment.
  • Low-Low (Coldspot): Low values surrounded by low values. Priority for structural humanitarian aid.
  • High-Low (Outlier): High value island surrounded by poverty. Regional service hub.
  • Low-High (Outlier): Low value pocket inside affluent core. Urban slum requiring utility hookups.

False Discovery Control

Because Local Moran performs $n$ simultaneous hypothesis tests (9,308 tests), multiple testing adjustments (Bonferroni or False Discovery Rate FDR) are applied to avoid spurious cluster identification.

Theoretical Foundations

Linear Regression vs. OLS: The Essential Distinction

In quantitative science, researchers often conflate the model with the estimator:

Linear Regression (The Model Class)

Linear Regression is a mathematical specification postulating that the conditional expectation of $y$ given $X$ is a linear combination of unknown parameters $\beta$:

$$y = X\beta + \epsilon, \quad E[y | X] = X\beta$$

It describes the data-generating process and functional form.

Ordinary Least Squares (The Estimator)

OLS is one specific computational optimization method that finds $\hat{\beta}$ by minimizing the sum of squared vertical residuals:

$$\min_\beta \sum_{i=1}^n e_i^2 \implies \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$$

Alternative estimators for the exact same linear model include: Maximum Likelihood (MLE), Weighted Least Squares (WLS), Generalized Least Squares (GLS), and 2SLS / Instrumental Variables.

Key Takeaway: When spatial dependence or heterogeneity occurs, the linear relationship $y = X\beta + \epsilon$ may still hold, but OLS is no longer the correct estimator—we switch to Maximum Likelihood (SAR/SEM) or Local Weighted Least Squares (GWR)!

Classical Regression

The 5 Gauss-Markov Assumptions & the BLUE Theorem

For OLS to be the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE), all five conditions must hold strictly:

  1. Linearity in Parameters: $y = X\beta + \epsilon$.
  2. Strict Exogeneity: Error has zero conditional mean: $E[\epsilon | X] = 0$.
  3. Full Column Rank: No perfect multicollinearity ($\text{rank}(X) = p + 1$).
  4. Homoskedasticity: Constant error variance: $\text{Var}(\epsilon_i | X) = \sigma^2$.
  5. Uncorrelated Disturbances: Zero error covariance across observations: $$\text{Cov}(\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j) = 0 \quad \text{for all } i \neq j$$

The Inevitable Spatial Breakdown

In spatial and geographic data, Assumption #5 is fundamentally violated by Tobler's First Law! Neighboring administrative wards share topography, climate, culture, and unmeasured infrastructure.

When Assumption #5 breaks, OLS is no longer BLUE: its variance is not minimal, and standard errors are heavily distorted.

Pre-Modeling Diagnostics

Pre-Modeling Diagnostics: Multicollinearity, VIF & Condition Number

Before running spatial econometrics, we must verify that explanatory predictors $X$ are not mutually redundant:

$$\text{VIF}_k = \frac{1}{1 - R_k^2}, \quad \text{Condition Number } \kappa(X) = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}(X'X)}{\lambda_{\min}(X'X)}}$$

VIF Value Ranges & Prescribed Action

  • $\text{VIF} = 1.0$: Ideal. Predictor is orthogonal to other features.
  • $1.0 \lt \text{VIF} \lt 5.0$: Low / Safe collinearity. Models are stable.
  • $5.0 \le \text{VIF} \lt 10.0$: Moderate concern. Scrutinize standard errors.
  • $\text{VIF} \ge 10.0$ or $\kappa \gt 30$: Severe collinearity. Matrix is near-singular; must drop or combine variables.

Real-Life Applied Use Case

Urban Density vs Market Access: In Nigerian wards, raw population count and building footprint counts correlate at $r=0.88$. Combining them into per-10k facility rates lowers VIF from 14.2 down to 1.84, ensuring stable regression coefficients!

Diagnostic Tests

Econometric Residual Diagnostics: Jarque-Bera & Breusch-Pagan

Standard OLS inference requires assessing residual normality and homoskedasticity:

Jarque-Bera (JB) Normality Test

$$\text{JB} = \frac{n}{6} \left( S^2 + \frac{(K - 3)^2}{4} \right) \sim \chi^2(2)$$

• $S$: Residual skewness (0 for normal).
• $K$: Residual kurtosis (3 for normal).
• $H_0$: Errors are normally distributed.
• Interpretation Range: If $p \lt 0.05$ ($\text{JB} \gt 5.99$), reject normality. In spatial data, extreme outliers create heavy tails, requiring robust estimators.

Breusch-Pagan / Koenker-Bassett Test

$$\text{BP} = \frac{1}{2} \text{ESS}_{\text{aux}} \sim \chi^2(p)$$

• Regresses squared residuals $e_i^2$ on predictors $X$.
• $H_0$: Error variance is constant ($\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2$).
• Interpretation Range: If $p \lt 0.05$, heteroskedasticity is present. Indicates that model error variance varies across space, signaling the need for GWR or spatial error modeling.

Spatial Breakdown

The Spatial Breakdown: Why Tobler's Law Breaks OLS Residuals

Because spatial units share geography, error terms across neighboring units covary strongly:

$$\text{Cov}(\epsilon_i, \epsilon_j) \neq 0 \quad \text{for neighboring units } i \sim j$$

Testing OLS residuals on Nigerian ward wealth models yields Residual Moran's $I = 0.482$ ($p \lt 0.0001$):

Omitted Spatial Lag Bias

If neighboring wealth spills over into focal ward wealth, OLS parameter estimates $\hat{\beta}$ are biased and inconsistent. OLS falsely credits local clinic presence with wealth created by regional trade!

Type-I Error Explosion

If unobserved regional shocks cluster spatially, OLS standard errors are severely underestimated, producing artificially high $t$-statistics and declaring non-existent policy interventions as statistically significant.

Model Selection

Model Selection: The Anselin LM Diagnostic Decision Tree

How do we mathematically decide which spatial model specification to deploy?

Step-by-Step Testing Procedure

  1. Fit the baseline OLS regression model: $y = X\beta + e$.
  2. Compute classical LM-Lag (tests $\rho = 0$) and LM-Error (tests $\lambda = 0$).
  3. If only one test is statistically significant ($p \lt 0.05$), select that model.
  4. If both are significant, compute Robust LM-Lag and Robust LM-Error (orthogonalized tests).

Exact Decision Rules

• Robust LM-Lag > Robust LM-Error → Deploy Spatial Lag Model (SAR).
• Robust LM-Error > Robust LM-Lag → Deploy Spatial Error Model (SEM).
• If both robust statistics remain highly significant → Estimate the Spatial Durbin Model (SDM).
• Always verify selection with the lowest AIC and highest Log-Likelihood.

Spatial Econometrics

Spatial Lag Model (SAR): Capturing Behavioral Spillovers

Incorporates the spatial lag of the dependent variable directly as an endogenous regressor:

$$y = \rho W y + X\beta + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)$$

Parameter $\rho$ & Value Range

  • Parameter $\rho$: Spatial autoregressive parameter.
  • Value Range: $-1 \lt \rho \lt 1$ (typically $0 \lt \rho \lt 1$ in socio-economic data).
  • Interpretation: $\rho$ quantifies direct peer spillover and behavioral contagion across borders.
  • Estimation: Estimated via Maximum Likelihood (MLE) or Instrumental Variables (2SLS).

Real-Life Applied Use Cases

  • Retail Geomarketing: High consumer spending in Ward $A$ attracts customers and delivery logistics into adjacent Ward $B$.
  • Epidemic Transmission: Infection outbreaks in Ward $A$ diffuse directly across borders into contiguous Ward $B$.
Policy Multiplier

The Spatial Multiplier: Direct, Indirect & Total Effects

In a spatial lag model, an investment in Unit $i$ radiates through neighboring feedback loops:

$$y = (I - \rho W)^{-1} X\beta + (I - \rho W)^{-1}\epsilon, \quad \text{Multiplier} = \frac{1}{1 - \rho}$$

Impact Decomposition

  • Direct Effect: Internal return on investment inside the focal target ward.
  • Indirect Effect (Spillover): Stimulus radiating into contiguous neighbor wards through geographic trade networks.
  • Total Effect: System-wide return ($2.41\times$ baseline when $\rho = 0.5842$).

Concrete Real-World Case ($\rho = 0.5842$)

A \$1,000,000 capital grant invested into wholesale commercial markets in a central hub ward produces \$1,000,000 in direct local wealth plus \$1,405,000 in indirect wealth across neighboring communities through geographic spillovers!

Spatial Econometrics

Spatial Error Model (SEM): Unobserved Regional Factors

Captures spatial dependence operating through unobserved regional variables and geographic noise:

$$y = X\beta + u, \quad u = \lambda W u + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)$$

Parameter $\lambda$ & Value Range

  • Parameter $\lambda$: Spatial error autoregressive coefficient.
  • Value Range: $-1 \lt \lambda \lt 1$.
  • Interpretation: Captures spatial clustering in omitted, unmeasured variables (environmental shocks, shared topography).
  • Policy Meaning: No direct behavioral spillovers; spatial correlation is an error process.

Real-Life Applied Use Cases

  • Agricultural Crop Yields: Unmeasured regional drought, groundwater table depth, or soil chemistry affecting all contiguous farms.
  • Public Utilities: Shared regional electric grid outages or state-level water procurement delays affecting all wards in a river basin.
Spatial Econometrics

Spatial Durbin Model (SDM): Endogenous & Contextual Spillovers

Nests both endogenous outcome lag ($Wy$) and exogenous predictor spillovers ($WX$):

$$y = \rho Wy + X\beta + WX\gamma + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)$$

Dual Parameter Interpretation

  • $\rho$ (Endogenous Spillover): Neighboring outcome $y$ spilling over into focal $y$.
  • $\gamma$ (Contextual Spillover): Neighboring characteristics $X$ directly impacting focal $y$.
  • Global Nesting: Reduces to SAR if $\gamma = 0$; reduces to SEM if $\gamma = -\rho \beta$.

Real-Life Applied Use Case

Education & Healthcare Capital Planning: If a neighboring ward builds a secondary school or hospital ($WX$), its physical presence directly improves living standards in your ward ($\gamma$), while their rising overall health ($Wy$) further stimulates your community ($\rho$).

Local Modeling

Global vs. Local: Why Global Models Miss Spatial Heterogeneity

All global models (OLS, SAR, SEM, SDM) operate under the assumption of spatial stationarity:

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{i1} + \dots + \beta_p X_{ip} + \epsilon_i \implies \beta_k \text{ is constant across every square kilometer}$$

The Spatial Heterogeneity Reality

In real-world geographic processes, the relationship between $X$ and $y$ changes across space:

  • In northern rural savannah wards, healthcare clinic provision has a massive marginal effect on reducing child mortality.
  • In dense southern metropolitan wards, clinics are already saturated, and traffic congestion or sanitation is the binding constraint.

The Methodological Solution

We transition from global regression to local spatial regression: Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) and Multiscale GWR (MGWR).

Local Regression

Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR): Theory & Math

GWR allows regression parameters to vary locally at each geographic coordinate $(u_i, v_i)$:

$$y_i = \beta_0(u_i, v_i) + \sum_{k=1}^p \beta_k(u_i, v_i) x_{ik} + \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$$

Local Estimation via Weighted Least Squares

At each target ward $i$, parameter vector $\hat{\beta}(u_i, v_i)$ is solved using local spatial weights $W(u_i, v_i)$:

$$\hat{\beta}(u_i, v_i) = \left( X' W(u_i, v_i) X \right)^{-1} X' W(u_i, v_i) y$$

Observations near ward $i$ receive higher weights; distant observations receive near-zero weights.

Real-Life Applied Use Case

Subsidized Seed & Fertilizer Distribution: In arid northern wards, agricultural fertilizer yields a $\hat{\beta} = +2.40$ boost in crop output; in wetland coastal wards, excess fertilizer causes runoff with $\hat{\beta} = -0.15$. GWR reveals these vital local policy variations!

Bandwidth Selection

GWR Spatial Kernels & Bandwidth Selection

The kernel weighting function $w_{ij}$ and its bandwidth $b$ dictate how local influence decays over distance:

Continuous vs. Adaptive Kernels

  • Continuous Gaussian Kernel: $$w_{ij} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d_{ij}}{b}\right)^2\right)$$ Fixed distance $b$ across all points. Fails in sparse rural areas.
  • Adaptive Bisquare Kernel (Recommended): $$w_{ij} = \begin{cases} \left[1 - \left(\frac{d_{ij}}{b_i}\right)^2\right]^2 & \text{if } d_{ij} \lt b_i \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$ Bandwidth $b_i$ expands in sparse rural wards and shrinks in dense urban wards!

Bandwidth Optimization via AICc

Golden section search optimizes bandwidth $b$ by minimizing the Corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc):

• Small Bandwidth: High local flexibility, but excessive variance and potential overfitting.
• Large Bandwidth ($b \to \infty$): Low variance, but approaches the global OLS model (underfitting).

Multiscale Modeling

Multiscale GWR (MGWR): Processes Operating at Multiple Scales

Standard GWR enforces the assumption that all $p$ relationships operate at the exact same spatial bandwidth:

$$y_i = \beta_{bw_0}(u_i, v_i) + \sum_{k=1}^p \beta_{bw_k}(u_i, v_i) x_{ik} + \epsilon_i \implies \text{Variable-specific bandwidths } bw_k$$

The Scale Spectrum in MGWR

  • Micro-Scale (Small Bandwidth, e.g. $k=45$): Hyper-local processes (e.g. corner store retail competition, neighborhood crime).
  • Meso-Scale (Medium Bandwidth, e.g. $k=400$): Regional processes (e.g. state health ministry policies, river basin hydrology).
  • Macro-Scale (Global Bandwidth, $k \to n$): Broad spatial processes (e.g. national inflation, currency exchange rates).

Real-Life Applied Use Case

Epidemiological Policy Planning: MGWR proves that clinic accessibility operates at a local micro-scale ($k=52$ wards), while annual rainfall operates at a regional meso-scale ($k=850$ wards), and national health insurance operates at a global scale ($k=9,308$ wards)!

Local Diagnostics

Interpreting GWR & MGWR Local Parameter Outputs

Unlike global regressions that produce a single summary table, GWR yields spatial data surfaces:

Key Diagnostic Outputs

  • Local $R^2$ Maps: Illustrates where the model performs exceptionally ($R^2 \gt 0.70$) vs where key local factors are missing ($R^2 \lt 0.20$).
  • Local Parameter Surfaces $\hat{\beta}_k(u_i, v_i)$: Maps showing the changing magnitude and direction of policy impacts.
  • Local $t$-Statistics: Surfaces of $t_i = \frac{\hat{\beta}_{ki}}{\text{SE}_{ki}}$. Only regions where $|t_i| \gt 1.96$ are deemed locally statistically significant.

Model Diagnostic Evaluation

• AICc Reduction: A drop of $\gt 3$ points over OLS confirms superior explanatory performance.
• Residual Autocorrelation: GWR eliminates spatial autocorrelation in residuals (Moran's $I \to 0$), verifying that local spatial structure has been fully captured.
